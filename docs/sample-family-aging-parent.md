## Getting started

Susan Hale, daughter. Writing about Eleanor Hale, who goes by Ellie to her
friends and Mom to me. Address: 34 Chestnut Lane, Vienna, VA.

## About Mom

**Who she is.** Mom is 79, a retired school librarian, and the person half
her street still calls before they call anyone else. She reads two books a
week and has opinions about both. She has buried a husband and a sister and
come to more life than most people start with. She is funny in a way that
sneaks up on you, and she will feed anyone who stands still on her porch.

**The history that shaped her.** She grew up in Roanoke, third of six. She
ran the school library on Maple Avenue for thirty-one years and there are
grown adults in this town who still stop her at the pharmacy to tell her
what they're reading. Dad died in 2019. The garden got bigger that year,
which is how she talks about things she does not talk about.

**Temperament, and how she takes help.** She will accept a ride but not an
arm. Offer to carry her bag and she'll hand it over cheerfully; take it out
of her hand and you have started something you will not win. She would
rather be twenty minutes late than be hurried, and she considers being
managed a form of rudeness.

**Hearing, vision, memory, as they are now.** Hearing aids in both ears;
she forgets the left one, and it's in the ashtray she never used, by the
front door. Reading glasses on a chain. Her long memory is a library; this
week is where the gaps are. She repeats a question sometimes an hour apart,
covers it beautifully, and would rather cover than ask. If she calls you
"honey" mid-sentence she has lost your name and is buying time; just work
your name into your next sentence and no harm done.

**What she will not admit to needing.** The stairs. Watch her pause at the
landing and count. Help in the shower is a conversation we have not won
yet. And money: she will never say a bill confused her, but the stack on
the hall table tells you.

**What she cannot abide.** Being talked about in the third person while
she is in the room. Baby talk. Anyone who says "we" when they mean her, as
in "how are we today." The television above volume 20, which is a fight
about the hearing aids in disguise.

**What strangers get wrong.** They hear the hearing aids and start
shouting. She hears fine if you face her and speak normally. Shouting
makes it worse and embarrasses her, and she will quietly decide you are a
fool.

## A typical week

**Mornings.** Up at six, robe, coffee: half a cup, milk, no sugar, in Dad's
mug, and do not offer her a different mug. Pills from the Tuesday-labeled
organizer on the sill. Then the crossword. Do not raise anything real
before the crossword.

**Evenings.** Supper at six because it was always at six. Jeopardy at 7:30,
out loud, competitively. Phone call with David on Sundays at 4; she is by
the phone at 3:45 pretending she isn't.

**The fixed points.** Monday: Meals on Wheels route with June, Mom
navigating, which is the job she will not surrender. Wednesday: hair at
Dot's, 10 a.m. Thursday: Giant, same list, same order, same cashier if
Marlene is on. Sunday: 9 a.m. Mass, then the cemetery in good weather.

**Getting around.** She still drives, daytime only, five familiar routes,
and the car is a sovereignty issue, not a transportation one. She has
agreed, twice, not to drive at night or in rain. Verify gently. This is
the unfinished conversation of our family and it must be handled like a
border negotiation.

**Food.** She cooks breakfast and lunch honestly. Supper "cooking" has
quietly become toast unless someone joins her, so I bring dinners Sundays,
labeled by day, and we maintain the fiction that it's because I "made too
much." Appetite is fine in company. Dentures out at night, upper only.

**A good day** has the garden in it, and she'll call me about what the
roses are doing. **A hard day** follows a bad night or a doctor's letter
in the mail; she goes short with people and won't say why. Tea, the
garden, and no big subjects until tomorrow.

## Talking with Mom

**How to raise things, and who from.** Straight, once, sitting down, no
television. She can smell a rehearsed speech from the driveway. Anything
about money lands better from David; anything about health, from me; and
anything about driving, honestly, from Ruth Ann, who she considers an
equal and not a child with opinions.

**The unfinished conversations.** Night driving: agreed, mostly holding.
The stairs and a first-floor bedroom: raised twice, refused twice, and
the second refusal had a reason in it, which is in the never-change
section. More help in the house: she'll take "company," she won't take
"help," so the cleaner who comes Fridays is officially "company who tidies."

**What helps.** Giving her the decision to make, out loud, every time.
"What do you want to do about the gutters, Mom" gets the gutters fixed.
"I've arranged the gutters" gets the arrangement cancelled.

**What to avoid.** Never open with "we've decided." Never say "facility"
in any sentence in this house. Do not compare her to her sister, who went
into memory care at the end; it is her exact fear and she will end the
conversation and possibly the visit.

## Health and medical

**Conditions.** Atrial fibrillation, 2018, on a blood thinner. Osteoarthritis,
both knees. The memory changes above: her doctor's word so far is "mild,"
and we test yearly.

**Allergies.** Sulfa drugs: full-body rash. It's on file everywhere, and
on the card in her wallet.

**Medications.** Warfarin, 5 mg, evenings, the one that must never be
missed or doubled; the organizer is the system of record, not her memory
and not mine. Metoprolol mornings. Tylenol for the knees; she calls
anything stronger "a fuss."

**Providers.** Dr. Alan Reyes, family medicine, (555) 016-7702, who she
likes because he talks books first and blood pressure second. Cardiology:
Dr. Osei, quarterly, (555) 016-7748.

**Pharmacy.** Main Street Pharmacy; they deliver Thursdays and Amir calls
her by name.

**Preferred hospital.** County General. Her records, her cardiologist,
and her opinion of their coffee are all on file there.

**How appointments actually work.** She'll say the appointment went fine
and remember none of the middle. Someone goes in with her and writes
things down; the notebook is in the car door pocket. Dr. Reyes knows to
call me after anything that matters.

**Where the cards and records live.** Insurance cards in her wallet.
The medication list is taped inside the kitchen cabinet by the phone.
The grey file box in the hall closet holds the directives; the hospital
has copies.

## The emergency plan

**If she falls:** do not stand her straight up. Sit with her, check for
wrist and hip pain, and call Susan. If there is any confusion beyond her
normal, any head knock, or she cannot bear weight, call 911 first. She is
on a blood thinner; a hit to the head is an emergency even when she says
it isn't, and she will say it isn't.

**Call 911 when:** chest pain, trouble breathing, a fall with a head
knock, or confusion that is new. **Otherwise call** Susan, (555) 016-4410,
any hour, and Ruth Ann rings the doorbell in the meantime.

## Work and obligations

**Volunteering.** The Meals on Wheels route, Mondays, with June driving.
She is the navigator and the conversation, and both matter to the people
on that route. The parish library cart, which is hers alone.

**Commitments others rely on.** The route. The cart. And the Tuesday
phone call to her brother Walt in Richmond, who is 84 and counts on it.

**Who to tell.** June: (555) 016-5533. The parish office. Walt, gently.

**What to wind down, and what to keep.** The route can pass to June's
granddaughter, who has ridden along. The cart she would want continued
by someone who will actually read the books first. The Walt call cannot
be wound down; someone in this family makes that call, forever.

## Home and daily living

**The house.** Hers outright, forty-one years. Water shut-off behind the
dryer. The good spare key lives with Ruth Ann, not under the frog. The
alarm code is Dad's birthday, and she will not change it, and we have
stopped asking.

**What is deferred, and what is urgent.** The gutters, chronically. The
back porch rail is the urgent one: it moves, she uses it, and she has
declined to consider it a problem. Fix it while she is at Dot's and
accept the scolding.

**Help with the household.** She thinks she still does her own laundry.
I do it Saturdays while she naps. She cooks what she cooks; the freezer
dinners cover the rest. Franny "the company" tidies Fridays.

**Personal care.** She manages, slowly, and the shower is the risk: there
is a grab bar now (installed as a "towel rail," which is what diplomacy
looks like) and a mat. Hair is Dot's job, nails she does herself with the
radio on, and doing them is a good sign; a week of undone nails is
information.

**Pets and plants.** Biscuit, the terrier, eleven, fed at 7 and 5, walked
to the corner and back three times a day, vet on Elm. The roses are not
pets but try telling her that; if she ever cannot garden, bring the garden
to the porch in pots before anyone suggests giving it up.

**Safety.** No rugs on the stairs anymore. Night lights in the hall. The
alert pendant exists and lives in the fruit bowl, unworn; the argument
was fought and lost, so Ruth Ann and the curtain schedule are the actual
system. The stove has a kettle whistle rule: if the whistle isn't on the
kettle, it isn't on the stove.

## Money and documents

**Who handles the bills.** She does, mostly, with drift. David reads the
statements monthly from Portland by arrangement with her, which she calls
"David's little hobby."

**How the bills get paid.** Utilities and phone on autopay. Property tax
comes by mail twice a year and has been missed twice; David has the dates
flagged. The church pledge she writes by hand every January and would not
dream of automating.

**Where the money comes in.** Dad's pension on the first, Social Security
on the third. (Retirement income; there are no disability benefits in
play, and no trust. David's POA and the will are the machinery.)

**Scams and pressure.** The phone charities have her number and she
cannot hang up on a polite voice. After the roof-repair deposit incident
of 2023 (four hundred dollars, never seen again), the rule she agreed to
is: nothing over fifty dollars by phone without telling David first,
"because he enjoys it." Watch the hall table for pledge envelopes.

**Where the documents are kept.** The grey file box, hall closet: deed,
will, POAs, directives, the long-term-care policy David pays. Safe
deposit box at the Main Street branch; David is on the signature card.
Where, not what: no numbers live in this letter.

## Legal and decisions

**Powers of attorney.** Financial: David, effective now, signed 2021.
Medical: Susan, with David as alternate. Originals in the grey box, copies
with the lawyer and the hospital.

**Advance directive.** Signed 2021. Comfort first; she has said it plainly
and it is in writing. The hospital has it on file.

**Who decides what, in practice.** Everything day to day is hers, full
stop. Money over about five hundred dollars she talks over with David
first, by her own choice and her own phrasing: "I keep him busy." Medical
she wants explained to her and decided with me in the room, not for her.

**Professionals.** Claire Kelly at Trusts & Wealth did the POAs and the
will. Ruth Meyer does the taxes and has for twenty years.

## For whoever steps in

**The first week.** Call Dr. Reyes' office and say you are taking over
the notebook. Then Ruth Ann, who knows more than this letter. Keep Franny
coming Fridays; she is the only visitor Mom doesn't tidy for, which makes
her the only one Mom fully relaxes around. Walk Biscuit yourself before
you rearrange anything about Biscuit. The money can wait two weeks; David
has it.

**In hindsight.** I spent a year correcting her: the repeated questions,
the wrong names. It bought me nothing and cost us both. Answer the
question again like it's the first time; it is, for her. And I wish we
had put the grab bar in two years before we did. Do the safety things
early and call them something else.

**What must never change.** The upstairs bedroom, however sensible the
downstairs one looks on paper. The stairs are the only exercise she gets,
and that room is the one she shared with Dad for thirty-eight years; she
is not being stubborn, she is being loyal. Supper at six. Mass at nine.
The Walt call. Jeopardy out loud.

**Consult before anything irreversible.** Susan and David together, Dr.
Reyes on capacity, and Father Bright, who she has trusted with things she
has never told us. Selling the house is not a decision anyone makes in a
week from a hospital hallway; that sentence is why this letter exists.

## Faith, joy, and community

**Faith.** St. Luke's, 9 a.m. Mass, third pew, right side. Communion at
home on the first Friday if she cannot get out; Father Bright already
knows. Grace before dinner, always, even over toast.

**Her people.** Ruth Ann. June. Marlene at the Giant. Dot. The route
regulars, who she would want checked on if she ever can't.

**Traditions.** Christmas Eve is her house, ham, and the good tablecloth,
non-negotiable even if the guest list is two people and a terrier. The
lake cabin the first week of August, on the porch with a stack of books.

**What still brings her joy.** The roses. The crossword in pen. Biscuit's
opinions. A grandchild on the porch swing with nowhere else to be. The
third cup of coffee she has been told not to have.

## Final wishes

Beside Dad at St. Luke's; the plot is bought and the deed is in the grey
box. The Mass is planned in a letter she wrote Father Bright herself,
which is very like her. Organ donation: recorded at the DMV, "whatever
still works, honey." No fuss, real flowers, and the reception at the
parish hall because the house should not be sad for the neighbors.

## A personal message

**To whoever cares for her.** She spent her whole life being the person
who noticed everyone else. Notice her back. Laugh at the sneaky jokes.
Lose at Jeopardy occasionally; she knows when you're doing it and enjoys
the courtesy anyway.

**To David.** Reading the statements matters more than you think it does,
and so do the Sunday calls. You are not the far-away son. You are the
other half of this.

**To Mom.** You taught a whole town to read for pleasure and me to do
hard things without making them look hard. Whatever the coming years
take, they cannot take what you already did. The roses will be looked
after. So will you.
